The goal

Measure height from a detection

A camera in the cab looks up at the pantograph and the overhead line. If a model finds the pantograph, masts, arms and contact points reliably, the height of those elements can be measured from the detection.

Why plain boxes

For that measurement the top edge of a box has to stay parallel to the bottom of the frame. I tried rotated boxes, then dropped them: they cost much more labelling effort and gave nothing back for the measurement.

Nov 2025 to Jul 2026 at a glance

From a proof of concept to two trained detectors.

Nov – Dec 2025

Proof of concept and a big plan

I used a public Roboflow contact-point dataset to check that contact points could be detected in video at all. None of those images are in my dataset.

The first plan was ambitious: a custom attention network with depth input, and a stack of measurements on top. It was the starting point, not the destination.

Dec 2025 – Apr 2026

Depth exploration

I tried stereo and monocular depth (a ZED 2i camera, Isaac Sim synthetic scenes, Depth Anything) to see whether annotation and measurement could be automated. A real-time detection and measurement system was built and run on trains in Lucknow; the code isn't published, but here is where it ran.

The depth work didn't end up in the final labelling method, but it taught me what the camera could and couldn't resolve.

May 2026

Measuring wire height with depth

With the ZED mounted in Lucknow, I compared two ways of getting depth: the camera's own, and Fast-FoundationStereo, with a detector finding the wires in each frame. The overlay shows each tracked wire segment, its height from both sources, and the gaps where wires cross.

A detection with a depth reading, pantograph at 0.93 confidence, is the end of that chain.

May 2026

The rotated-box detour

I tried oriented boxes, including a depth-assisted script that proposed rotated pantograph boxes, which I calibrated against my own corrections. It matched my edits well, but I decided rotation wasn't needed and went back to plain boxes.

Knowing when to drop an idea saved weeks of labelling.

May – Jun 2026

The labelling campaign

A cheaper method won: a small pretrained detector pre-annotates, tracking carries boxes between frames, and I review every frame by hand in CVAT. 31 tasks covered all 23,551 frames from five routes.

Footage came from four of the five routes in this folder of raw videos, and the model-assisted tools sped up each pass without replacing the review.

10 – 23 Jun 2026

Training, and the NaN scare

I built the YOLO dataset on 10 June and the RF-DETR set on 12 June from the same labels, then trained. The first YOLO epochs went to NaN loss; Ultralytics logged "recovering from last.pt" and I resumed the run. At about 57 minutes an epoch, 200 epochs took roughly 8 days.

RF-DETR Large trained on a rented H100 in about 19 hours, finishing 30 June.

How the labelling was run

CVAT ran on my own PC and was reachable both locally and from off-site through an ngrok tunnel.

  • 31 tasks of about 500 frames, taken one at a time.
  • Two seats on every task: a labeller, and a reviewer who follows behind correcting mistakes.
  • One person as admin and host, switching between the two seats.

Data work

What's still imperfect

I'd rather you hear it from me.

  • Contact points are the weak spot. Both models score only about 0.39 AP@50:95 on them, against 0.90 or more for the pantograph. The boxes are around 1% of the frame width, so a few pixels of offset costs most of the overlap, and they are hard to place consistently by hand.
  • The two scores are not comparable. YOLO and RF-DETR were scored on different image sets, and RF-DETR's validation and test splits share video segments, so its numbers are likely optimistic. There is no clean held-out test set.
  • Neighbouring frames are near-duplicates, so the number of distinct scenes is far smaller than the 23,551 frames. The no-shared-segment guarantee covers video-derived frames only, not the augmented sources.
  • A narrow domain. Five routes, a webcam plus one ZED camera, and a limited range of weather and times of day. Expect a gap on other railways or camera mountings, and the ZED frames look quite different from the webcam ones.
  • Masts and arms are middling (about 0.69 to 0.79), and the pantograph is the only class above 0.9.
  • No agreement measure. Review was a second pass in CVAT, not an independent labelling, so there is no inter-annotator agreement score.

The full dataset (23,551 frames, 8.13 GB) is private and not available for download. The Kaggle write-up has sample frames, the full numbers, and the data-prep steps.
